The pivot from last week

WEEK 3 · A QUESTION ABOUT ROWS

“Can the adversary tell which record is Alice?”

Defended by making records look alike: generalise the quasi-identifiers until every pattern occurs \(k\) times.

Broke when a whole equivalence class shared one sensitive value. Ambiguity about identity is not privacy of the attribute.

WEEK 4 · A QUESTION ABOUT PARTICIPATION

“Would this output have been about as likely if Alice were not in the data at all?”

Defended by randomising the mechanism, so no output is much more likely under one dataset than under its neighbour.

Says nothing about rows, quasi-identifiers, or what the adversary already knows — which is exactly why it survives them.

\[ \text{Dalenius (1977): } \textit{nothing learnable from the release should be learnable without it} \;\longrightarrow\; \text{provably impossible} \]
Lecture 5's key idea: not revealing information about individuals is impossible — a useful statistic must reveal something. So bound the harm of participation instead: whatever is learned would have been learned regardless of whether you joined.
Use the Cate Blanchett example and the smoking example from lecture 5. Both show that a release can change what the world believes about a person without that person's participation being the cause. Dalenius's requirement rules out useful statistics entirely; Dwork and Naor's 2010 impossibility result is the formal version. The reframing to "harm of participation" is the whole intellectual move of the week — spend a minute on it.
1

The table you generalise

Finding a 3-anonymous table is a search problem. Incognito's job is to avoid searching.

Worksheet question 1 · Lecture 4
Set up: we are given a 12-row table, three domain generalisation hierarchies, and a target of k = 3. The naive approach scans every combination of generalisation levels. Incognito uses two structural facts to skip most of them.
60-second recap · lectures 3a, 3b & 4

The vocabulary Question 1 assumes you have

THREE KINDS OF COLUMN

Key attributes — name, address, phone number. Uniquely identifying, and always removed before release.

Quasi-identifiers — e.g. (postcode, birth date, sex). Not identifying on their own, but a combination an adversary may already hold from another source.

Sensitive attribute — what you are trying to protect. In Question 1 that is Loan taken.

WHY REMOVING NAMES IS NOT ENOUGH

Sweeney's Massachusetts breach: a de-identified medical release joined to the public voter list on ZIP + birth date + sex re-identified the state Governor.

87% of the US population is uniquely identified by those three fields alone.

Any attribute you add to a quasi-identifier set gives another quasi-identifier set — so the list only ever grows.

K-ANONYMITY · SAMARATI & SWEENEY

Each released record cannot be distinguished from at least k − 1 others in the release.

Equivalently: every combination of quasi-identifier values occurs in at least k records. Such a group is an equivalence class.

Reached by generalisation (replace a value with a coarser one) and suppression (drop it entirely).

A domain generalisation hierarchy is just the ladder of coarser domains for one attribute — 3000 → 30* → 3*. Question 1 hands you one ladder per quasi-identifier and asks for the least generalised combination that still reaches k = 3.
Thirty seconds each. The point students most often miss is the last line of the middle card — the quasi-identifier set is not fixed by the data, it is fixed by what else exists in the world, which is why lecture 4 says Q + {A'} is a quasi-identifier whenever Q is. Everything in part 1 is a search over generalisations of the quasi-identifiers only; the sensitive column is never generalised.
QUESTION 1 · THE SET-UP

Twelve records, three ladders, one target

THE QUASI-IDENTIFIERS AND THEIR HIERARCHIES

AttributeLevel 0Level 1Level 2
postcode30*, 31*, 3[2,4,5]*3[0,1]*, 3[2,4,5]*3*
age21-30, 31-40, 41+21-30, 31+*
income0-49K, 50-99K, 100K+*—

\(3[2,4,5]*\) means any code starting 32, 34 or 35.

WHAT MAKES THE SEARCH EXPENSIVE

Three ladders of height 3, 3 and 2 give \(3 \times 3 \times 2 = 18\) full-domain generalisations. Testing one means a full pass over the data to count equivalence classes.

Finding the least generalised table satisfying \(k\) is NP-hard in general (Meyerson & Williams, PODS 2004). Incognito does not solve it by being clever about the optimum — it solves it by never visiting most of the lattice.

Before the lab, predict: which single attribute do you expect to fail 3-anonymity first, and why? Look at the income column of the worksheet table.
Let the class read the data. Incomes are 100, 70, 60, 80, 110, 40, 55, 80, 30, 50, 55, 45 — bucketed at level 0 that is 3 / 7 / 2, and the class of size 2 (the two six-figure earners) already kills 3-anonymity. That single observation removes half the lattice before the search reaches two dimensions, which is the lesson of the lab.

The two facts Incognito runs on

SUBSET PROPERTY · PRUNES ACROSS DIMENSIONS

\[ D \text{ is } k\text{-anon wrt } Q \;\Longrightarrow\; D \text{ is } k\text{-anon wrt every } P \subseteq Q \]

Dropping an attribute can only merge equivalence classes, never split them. Used backwards: if a table fails on income alone, every combination containing income fails too — so those nodes are never generated.

ROLLUP / MONOTONICITY · PRUNES UPWARD

\[ \langle P_0, A_0\rangle \text{ satisfies } k \;\Longrightarrow\; \text{everything above it does} \]

Generalising further can only merge classes. So the sweep runs bottom-up and stops at the first success on each branch: everything above is settled without another scan.

Both are one-directional, and they travel along different axes. A failure rules out every larger attribute set containing it — and says nothing about generalising further. A success rules out the need to test every more generalised table of the same attributes — and says nothing about larger attribute sets. Confusing the two is the standard exam mistake.
Draw the asymmetry explicitly on the board if it helps: dimension grows sideways, generalisation grows upward. Failure travels sideways (to bigger attribute sets); success travels upward (to coarser tables). The lattice picture in the lab colours these differently, and the trace log names which rule fired at each step.

Lab 1 · Run Incognito on the worksheet's table

Step through the real algorithm. Every k= number is counted from the 12 rows; nothing is pre-computed.

Step through the 1-attribute phase by hand first — three scans settle postcode and age, and two settle income, with I0 failing at a class of 2. Ask what that failure has just bought: every node containing income at level 0 is now dead, which is 9 of the 18 full-domain nodes. By the end the counter reads 13, not 9 — the extra four are the combinations whose postcode-and-age pair failed at two dimensions, pruned by the same subset property one level up. Then run to the end. Land on the two answers, ⟨P0,A2,I1⟩ and ⟨P2,A0,I1⟩ — the worksheet's own answer names exactly these, reached by the same reasoning. Finally push k to 4 and 5 and watch the surviving set shrink; at k = 5 the only survivors suppress nearly everything.

Lab 2 · The table it hands back, and what that cost

Two incomparable answers. Both are 3-anonymous; they are not equally useful, and neither protects the sensitive column.

Start on ⟨P0,A2,I1⟩ — postcodes survive at their finest useful level, but age and income are gone entirely. Then switch to ⟨P2,A0,I1⟩ — now age survives and location is gone. Ask which release a bank would prefer, and make the point that "minimal" is a partial order, not a ranking: Incognito returns a set of incomparable answers and someone still has to choose. Then look at the loan column: some classes are homogeneous, so week 3's attack works on this table too. That is the bridge to part 2.

Where this approach runs out

IT PROTECTS IDENTITY, NOT ATTRIBUTES

A class of \(k\) records that all took the loan tells you the answer without telling you the row. Week 3's homogeneity attack, still working.

IT DEPENDS ON NAMING THE QUASI-IDENTIFIERS

Which columns are “identifying” depends on what external data exists — today and in five years. The guarantee is only as good as that guess.

IT DOES NOT COMPOSE

Two independently 3-anonymous releases of the same people can be intersected. Nothing in the definition bounds what happens next.
So change the object being protected. Not “which row is you”, but “whether you are here at all” — a property of the mechanism, which composes, needs no quasi-identifier list, and holds against any auxiliary knowledge.
Tie all three failures to the requirements list on lecture 5 slide 8: resilience to background knowledge, privacy without obscurity, post-processing, and graceful composition. k-anonymity satisfies none of the four in general. Differential privacy was designed against that list, and we will check each one off during parts 2 and 4.
2

The guarantee you claim

One inequality, quantified over every neighbouring pair and every output. Both quantifiers do real work.

Worksheet questions 2, 5 & 6 · Lectures 5 and 6
Part 2 is about reading the definition precisely enough to use it as a proof obligation — which is exactly what questions 5 and 6 ask for.
60-second recap · lecture 6

Why the mechanism has to be random at all

1 · RETURN f(D) EXACTLY

Unless \(f\) ignores the data entirely, some neighbouring pair gives two different answers. Take \(O\) to be one of them: probability 1 under one dataset, 0 under the other.

not DP for any \(\varepsilon\)

2 · RETURN A FIXED CONSTANT

Identical output distribution on every dataset, so the ratio is exactly 1.

0-DP — and useless

3 · IGNORE D AND RETURN NOISE

Also independent of the data, so also perfectly private, and carries no information about \(f\).

DP — and still useless
So the design problem is a trade, not a checkbox: stay close enough to \(f(D)\) to be worth publishing, while keeping the output distributions of every neighbouring pair overlapping enough that no output betrays which dataset you held. Parts 2 and 3 are the two halves of that sentence.
Lecture 6's "trivial mechanisms" sequence, slides 9–11. Worth doing out loud because it kills two intuitions at once: that determinism can ever be private, and that privacy alone is the goal. Note for the sharp student: returning f(D) exactly is DP when f is constant — the failure needs f to differ on some neighbouring pair. A dataset here is a multiset of records; two are neighbouring if they differ in at most one record.

The definition, and where each piece bites

\[ \Pr[M(D_1) \in O] \;\le\; e^{\varepsilon}\, \Pr[M(D_2) \in O] \qquad \text{for all } \|D_1 - D_2\| \le 1 \text{ and all } O \subseteq \mathcal{O} \]

FOR ALL NEIGHBOURING PAIRS

Not the pair you have — every pair differing in one record. This is what makes the guarantee independent of what the adversary already knows.

FOR ALL OUTPUT SETS

A single output set that violates the inequality is a counterexample. Question 6 is exactly this: find one \(O\).

RANDOMNESS OF \(M\) ONLY

The data is fixed; the probability is over the mechanism's coins. So \(M\) may be public, and only the noise draw must stay secret.
Read \(\varepsilon\) as a price, not a probability: it is the privacy cost of a query, and higher \(\varepsilon\) means less noise, more accuracy, and a weaker promise.
Stress the two universal quantifiers, because both questions in this part turn on them. Question 5 asks what a large ε means once the inequality is satisfied but toothless; question 6 asks students to exhibit one bad O. Note also that a deterministic non-constant mechanism can never be DP — which is lecture 6's "trivial attempts" sequence.

Lab 3 · Question 5 — what does \(\varepsilon = 50\) actually forbid?

The inequality is satisfied at every \(\varepsilon\). The question is what it still rules out.

Have the class predict before you move the slider: at ε = 1, how far can a 50% belief move? The answer, 26.9% to 73.1%, usually surprises people in both directions — it is a real shift, and it is bounded. Then jump to ε = 50 and let the band cover the axis. The worksheet's answer says the probability ratio is e^50 and that this is "unlikely to be produced by a neighbouring dataset"; make it concrete — e^50 is about 5 × 10²¹, so the bound forbids nothing any adversary would ever have believed. Finish on the Census preset: real deployments sit at ε values this picture calls useless, which is a genuine open tension, not a mistake.
QUESTION 6 · A PROOF OBLIGATION, BACKWARDS

“Draw a record at random and return it.” Show this is not \(\varepsilon\)-DP.

WHAT YOU HAVE TO PRODUCE

To prove DP you must show the inequality for every neighbouring pair and every \(O\). To disprove it you need one pair and one \(O\).

\[ \Pr[M(D)=\{d\}] = \tfrac{1}{|D|}, \qquad \Pr[M(D')=\{d\}] = 0 \]

WHY NO \(\varepsilon\) RESCUES IT

The requirement becomes \(\tfrac{1}{|D|} \le e^{\varepsilon} \cdot 0 = 0\), which is false for every finite \(\varepsilon\).

The failure is support, not scale: \(D\) can produce an output that \(D'\) simply cannot. Any mechanism that returns a genuine record has this shape.

The general test: if some output is possible under one dataset and impossible under a neighbour, no multiplicative bound can hold. Noise that fixes this must have full support — which is why Laplace, not a bounded uniform, is the mechanism of choice.
This is a short question but the reasoning generalises further than it looks. Ask the class which other "obvious" mechanisms fail this way: returning the maximum, returning the argmax record, returning any exact order statistic. Then ask what week 3's bounded noise in {−1, 0, 1} would do here — it is also finite-support, which is precisely why the certificate attack in week 3's lab 2 worked.

Lab 4 · Find the output that breaks it

Pick which record the neighbouring dataset drops, then compare the two output distributions.

Let a student choose the removed record; the witness moves with it, which makes the point that this is not a quirk of one record. Then switch the mechanism to the Laplace count on the same neighbouring pair: same two datasets, same difference of one person, but now every output is reachable from both and the ratio is capped at e^ε. Ask what changed — the answer is the support of the output distribution, not the amount of noise.
QUESTION 2 · NO TRUSTED CURATOR

Randomised response, and solving for \(\varepsilon = \ln 7\)

THE MECHANISM (WARNER 1965)

With probability \(p\) answer truthfully; otherwise report Yes or No uniformly at random. The respondent randomises before the data leaves them, so the curator never holds a true value.

THE CALCULATION

\[ \frac{\Pr[\mathsf{Yes} \mid \mathsf{Y}]}{\Pr[\mathsf{Yes} \mid \mathsf{N}]} = \frac{p + \tfrac{1}{2}(1-p)}{\tfrac{1}{2}(1-p)} = \frac{1+p}{1-p} = e^{\varepsilon} \]

Set \(\tfrac{1+p}{1-p} = 7\): then \(1+p = 7-7p\), so \(p = \tfrac{3}{4}\).

Sanity check the direction: \(p = \tfrac12\) is the lecture's double coin flip and gives \(\ln 3\). More truth means a larger ratio and a weaker guarantee, so \(\ln 7 > \ln 3\) must need \(p > \tfrac12\). It does.
Get the class to do the algebra before the lab, then check it on the slider. The direction check is worth labouring: students routinely solve the equation correctly and then assert that more privacy needs larger p. Note also that here a "dataset" is one person's single bit, so this is ε-DP for datasets of size 1 — local DP, and a strictly stronger trust model than central DP.

Lab 5 · Turn the privacy dial and watch utility pay

The estimator stays unbiased at every \(p\). Only its interval moves — that is the entire trade.

Press Solve for ε = ln 7 and confirm p lands on 0.75 with a ratio of exactly 7. Then drag p downward and watch two things at once: ε falls, and the 95% interval widens even though the point estimate stays centred. Show the estimator from lecture 6, x̂ = (Z/n − ½(1−p))/p, and note that its standard error carries a 1/p — that factor is the price of the lie. Finish by dropping n and asking how many respondents ln 7 would need for a ±1 point answer.

The price of removing the trusted curator is a \(\sqrt{n}\) factor in the error. That is why Chrome, iOS and Windows telemetry — hundreds of millions of respondents — can afford local DP, and a hospital with 500 patients cannot.
Lecture 6's comparison table. The √n is the punchline: local DP noise per person is O(1/ε) and the errors add in quadrature across n people, so the aggregate carries √n·O(1/ε) while central DP adds noise once. Connect back to the deployments on lecture 5 slide 11 — every local-DP deployment named there has a user base large enough to absorb it.

The noise you calibrate

The mechanism is fixed. All the design work is in one number.

Worksheet questions 3, 4 & 7 · Lecture 6
Parts 1 and 2 were about what to promise. Part 3 is the engineering: given the promise, how much noise, and how do you know?
60-second recap · lecture 6

The two definitions the next three questions use

THE LAPLACE DISTRIBUTION · DEFINITION 3.2

\[ \mathrm{Lap}(x \mid b) = \frac{1}{2b}\exp\!\left(-\frac{|x|}{b}\right), \qquad \sigma^2 = 2b^2 \]

Centred at 0, symmetric, and — unlike a bounded uniform — strictly positive everywhere. A larger scale \(b\) means a flatter curve: more privacy, less accuracy.

THE LAPLACE MECHANISM · DEFINITION 3.3

\[ \mathcal{M}_L(x, f, \varepsilon) = f(x) + (Y_1,\dots,Y_k), \quad Y_i \sim \mathrm{Lap}\!\left(\frac{\Delta f}{\varepsilon}\right) \]

Add one draw per output coordinate, scaled by sensitivity over \(\varepsilon\). Theorem: this is \(\varepsilon\)-differentially private, for any \(f\) returning real numbers.

Everything below is now mechanical except one number. The mechanism is fixed and the theorem is proved; questions 3, 4 and 7 differ only in what \(\Delta f\) turns out to be — and question 7 in whether it exists at all.
Point at the two places b appears: the scale is Δf/ε, so noise grows with sensitivity and shrinks with ε. The "strictly positive everywhere" line is the one to stress — it is exactly what question 6 shows a sampling mechanism lacks, and it is why bounded noise (like week 3's η ∈ {−1,0,1}) cannot give a DP guarantee however you tune it.

Sensitivity is a worst case, not an average

\[ \Delta f \;=\; \max_{\text{neighbours } D_1, D_2} \; \| f(D_1) - f(D_2) \|_1 \qquad\qquad M(D) = f(D) + Y, \quad Y \sim \mathrm{Lap}\!\left(\tfrac{\Delta f}{\varepsilon}\right) \]

Q3 · SUM OVER \([l, h]\)

Adding or removing one record moves the sum by at most \(h\).
\(\Delta f = h\), \(b = h/\varepsilon\)

Q3 · AVERAGE OVER \([l, h]\)

The same change, divided by \(n\).
\(\Delta f = h/n\), \(b = h/(n\varepsilon)\)

Q4 · ONE WIDER VALUE

\(v_1 \in [l, 2h]\) while the rest stay in \([l,h]\).
\(\Delta f = 2h\) — everyone pays
Say the assumption out loud: these answers take \(0 \le l < h\), so the largest change a single record can make is \(h\). If \(l\) were negative the sensitivity would be \(\max(|l|, h)\) instead. The worksheet works in the non-negative case.
The lecture derives Δf = 1 for a count and Δf ≤ 1/n for a mean of a 0/1 attribute (slides 15–17); these questions are the same argument with a general range. Question 4 is the one that catches people: the maximum is over neighbouring pairs, so the pair that removes v₁ sets the sensitivity for the whole release, even though only one record could ever be that large. Do the assumption note explicitly — it is a fair question from a sharp student and the answer sheet is silent on it.

Lab 6 · Questions 3 and 4 — calibrate the mechanism

The blue curve is the answer's distribution on \(D\); the dashed curve is the neighbour \(D'\). \(\Delta f\) is exactly how far apart they sit.

Start on sum and slide n from 5 to 200: the noise does not move, because a sum's sensitivity does not depend on n. Switch to average and repeat — now the noise collapses. That contrast is the whole of question 3. Then flip to the Q4 setting and watch both the sensitivity and the gap between the two curves double while nothing else changes. Ask the class what fraction of the dataset caused that: one record in n.

Concept check · one wide value

Expect the averaging answer to be popular. The correction is that sensitivity has no n in it for a sum — the definition maximises over pairs and never divides by anything.
QUESTION 7 · AN UNBOUNDED DOMAIN

Income starts at 0 and has no upper bound. Now what?

WHY LAPLACE CANNOT BE APPLIED AS IT STANDS

The mechanism needs \(\Delta f\), and \(\Delta f\) is a maximum over what one record can contribute. With no upper bound that maximum is infinite, so the scale \(\Delta f / \varepsilon\) is undefined. There is nothing to calibrate to.

THE FIX, AND WHY IT IS FREE TO PUBLISH

Choose a bound \(C\) in advance, clip every value to \([0, C]\), then compute. Now \(\Delta f = C/n\) for the mean.

\(C\) is a parameter of the mechanism, not a function of the data, so announcing it leaks nothing — and lecture 5's second requirement says the adversary is assumed to know it anyway.

What clipping costs: a downward bias on everyone above \(C\). Choose \(C\) too high and the noise swamps the answer; too low and the bias does. And bias, unlike noise, does not shrink as \(n\) grows.
The answer sheet gives the fix in one sentence; the lab prices it. Emphasise that C must not be chosen by looking at the data — picking C as the observed maximum is a data-dependent choice and leaks the maximum, which is exactly the statistic most likely to identify someone. If students ask how to choose C legitimately: from public knowledge, from a prior release's budget, or by spending a little ε on a private quantile estimate.

Lab 7 · Price the clipping bound

500 synthetic incomes with a long tail. Move \(C\) and watch bias and noise trade against each other.

Press Refuse to clip first so the class sees the failure mode the worksheet is describing: sensitivity set by the single richest person, noise far larger than the answer. Then walk C down and find the minimum. Two things to name: the total-error curve is U-shaped, so there is a genuine optimum; and the left branch is more dangerous than the right, because a biased answer still looks precise. Finish by dropping ε and watching the optimum move right — less privacy budget means you can afford to keep more of the tail.

The budget you spend

One release is a calculation. Many releases is an accounting problem.

Worksheet questions 8 & 9 · Lecture 6
Part 4 closes both the week and the two-week arc: composition is the property week 3's mechanisms lacked, and membership inference is the attack week 3 built.
60-second recap · lecture 6

The two properties that make \(\varepsilon\) an accounting unit

SEQUENTIAL COMPOSITION · QUESTION 9

\[ M(D) = \big(M_1(D), M_2(D)\big) \text{ is } (\varepsilon_1 + \varepsilon_2)\text{-DP} \]

Privacy degrades gracefully and, crucially, predictably. Costs add, so a curator can set a budget in advance and subtract from it per query.

Proof: multiply the two per-mechanism bounds on the joint density. Dwork & Roth, Theorem 3.14.

POST-PROCESSING

\[ f \circ M \text{ is } \varepsilon\text{-DP for any } f \text{ independent of } D \]

Thinking about a DP output cannot make it less private — regardless of the attacker's strategy or computational power. This is what lets data users do whatever they like with a release.

Compare with week 3. Suppression had no composition rule, so three safe answers combined into a name. Here the rule exists, it is additive, and when the budget is spent the curator must stop answering.
Question 9 is marked optional and the answer sheet points at Dwork and Roth's Theorem 3.14; sketch it rather than proving it. The idea in one line: for a fixed output pair, the joint density ratio factorises into the two per-mechanism ratios, each bounded by its own e^εᵢ, and the product is e^(ε₁+ε₂). Note also that this is the loose bound — advanced composition gives roughly √k·ε for k queries under (ε, δ)-DP, which is beyond this subject but worth naming.

Lab 8 · Spend the budget, and watch week 3's attack die

Fixed total \(\varepsilon\), split across \(k\) queries. Switch to “the same query \(k\) times” for the comparison that matters.

Set the strategy to the same query k times and raise k. In week 3's lab 2, repeating a noisy query drove the error down as 1/√k and the attack succeeded. Here each repetition must be paid for out of a fixed budget, so each answer carries k times the noise and averaging only removes √k of it — the net error rises like √k. Say the conclusion plainly: differential privacy is the first mechanism this term that does not get weaker the more you ask it. That is composition doing the work, not the noise distribution.
QUESTION 8 · DOES DP TRAINING STOP THE WEEK 3 ATTACK?

Treat the model as the output of a mechanism

THE ARGUMENT

If training is \(\varepsilon\)-DP with the usual neighbouring definition — two datasets differing in one record — then the trained model is a DP release. An adversary cannot tell which of two neighbouring training sets produced it, and “was this record in the training set?” is precisely that question.

Post-processing does the rest: querying the model is a function of its parameters, so no attack on it can beat the bound.

THE BOUND, AND ITS SMALL PRINT

\[ \text{membership advantage} \;\le\; e^{\varepsilon} - 1 \]

Yeom et al., CSF 2018 — the reference the answer sheet gives. Note the range: advantage is at most 1 by definition, so the bound says nothing once \(\varepsilon \ge \ln 2 \approx 0.69\).

So the honest answer is two-part: yes, DP provably bounds membership inference — and at the \(\varepsilon\) values people actually deploy, that particular theorem is vacuous. DP-SGD does defeat these attacks in practice, but that is an empirical finding, not this inequality.
The worksheet's answer is correct and worth reading aloud, including its caveat that the neighbourhood definition matters. Push one step further than the answer does: have the class compute e^ε − 1 at ε = 1. It is 1.72, which is no constraint at all on a quantity that cannot exceed 1. The gap between the provable bound and the observed robustness of DP-SGD is real and active research — Jayaraman and Evans 2019 and Nasr et al. 2021, both cited on lecture 6 slide 38, are exactly about this gap.

Lab 9 · Where the guarantee actually bites

The green curve is the bound. The dashed line is week 3's lab 5: 73% attack accuracy at the best threshold, i.e. advantage 2(0.73) − 1 = 0.46.

Press halve week 3: to cut the attack's advantage to half of its undefended level you need ε = ln(1.23) ≈ 0.21, and merely to beat it at all you need ε below ln(1.46) ≈ 0.38 — both far stricter than any deployed system uses. Then move to ε = 1 and note that the theorem has stopped constraining anything. The teaching point is about how to read a bound, not about DP being weak: a guarantee that is vacuous in the regime you deploy in is not evidence of safety, and you need the empirical result as well.

Concept check · what the guarantee does not say

Lecture 6's closing slide: DP does not create privacy where none existed, does not protect you from true population-level findings, and does not replace the policy question of whether the analysis should run at all.

Take-home principles

WHEN YOU READ A PRIVACY CLAIM

  • Ask what the guarantee quantifies over — which pairs, which outputs.
  • Convert \(\varepsilon\) into a belief shift before deciding it is small.
  • Check whether the bound is binding in the regime actually deployed.
  • A satisfied inequality that forbids nothing is not a guarantee.

WHEN YOU BUILD A MECHANISM

  • Derive \(\Delta f\) as a maximum over neighbours, never an average.
  • Bound the domain before you need to, and publish the bound.
  • Budget across the whole sequence of releases, not per query.
  • Never let a mechanism parameter depend on the data it protects.
